The story in short

What we know, in five paragraphs

The Spaenys are a Swiss family. The name was Späni, and it belongs to Canton Schwyz — the small, fiercely Catholic mountain canton that gave Switzerland its name and its flag. Within Schwyz the family's home was Illgau, a parish of scattered farms perched at 800 metres above the Muota valley, where Späni is recorded as one of the eight original families that made up the entire community. The earliest ancestor on the line, Andreas Späni, died in 1696; his descendants farmed Illgau's slopes for five generations, marrying into the neighbouring founding families of Bürgler and Heinzer.

In the middle of the nineteenth century Josef Anton Späni did something none of his ancestors had done: he left the mountains. By 1860 he was in Canton Thurgau, two cantons to the north-east, in the gentler orchard and textile country near Lake Constance. His son Karl Anton was born there, at Weinfelden, in 1861. Karl Anton lost his father at fifteen and his mother at eighteen, and within a few years of being orphaned he was on a ship to New York.

He arrived around 1880 — through Castle Garden, not Ellis Island, which did not yet exist — and by May 1882 he was in St. Louis marrying Sophia Fuhlendorf, a German immigrant from Schleswig-Holstein. They raised seven children in Brooklyn and Queens, calling two of the boys Charles Lincoln and Ernest Columbus. American clerks wrote the surname five different ways over the next fifty years; the version cut into his headstone in 1939 was Spaeny. By 1905 the family had moved to Rice County, Kansas, where Sophia died in 1914 and where Karl Anton — now Charles — was buried a quarter-century later. His younger brother Max had emigrated too, and settled in Los Angeles.

Charles's son Ernest Columbus Spaeny farmed Lincoln Township and raised two boys in Lyons. The younger, Donald Kenneth, was drafted into the Army ten weeks after his eighteenth birthday, in January 1944, and came home to marry Alberta Humphrey, sell industrial equipment for Stewart-Warner, and command the American Legion post in Hutchinson. His three sons are Michael, Mark and Matthew; Michael's son is Shawn, who commissioned this record, and Mark's daughter is the actress Cailee Spaeny.

What is still missing: the ship Karl Anton sailed on, the unit Donald served in and the medals he earned, the family's registered coat of arms in the Schwyz archives, and the Swiss parish registers that would turn the four earliest generations from a family tree into documented fact. The plan for finding each of them is at the bottom of this page.

Where we come from

Illgau, and the canton that named a country

Two places matter. Canton Schwyz is the political and religious world the family belonged to for at least two centuries. Illgau is the actual ground they farmed — and the community their name helped found.

Schwyz began as a community of free peasants at the foot of the two Mythen peaks, and in August 1291 it swore the Federal Charter with Uri and Unterwalden — the pact from which the Swiss Confederation grew. When Habsburg Austria tried to bring the valley to heel, Schwyz men ambushed and destroyed Duke Leopold's army at Morgarten in 1315. The confederacy that spread outward from those valleys took its name from this canton: Schwyz became Schweiz, Switzerland, and the canton's red banner with its small white cross became the national flag. Your ancestors' home canton is, literally, the one the country is named after.

Schwyz fought Zwingli's Protestants at Kappel in 1531 and won, and from then on it was one of the most stubbornly Catholic places in Europe — every Späni on this page was baptised, married and buried by a Catholic priest. The canton governed itself by Landsgemeinde, an open-air assembly of all male citizens, until the federal state ended the practice after the Sonderbund War of 1847, in which Schwyz fought on the losing Catholic side.

The great convulsion in the family's lifetime was 1798–99. When revolutionary France invaded Switzerland, Schwyz raised ten thousand men under Alois von Reding, won two battles, and still had to capitulate in May 1798. Eighteen months later the Russian general Suvorov marched his army over the Kinzig Pass into the Muota valley and fought the French there before escaping over the Pragel Pass to Glarus. That fighting happened directly below Illgau. Johann Werner Späni was twenty-five that autumn; his father Johann Josef had died in June, before the armies came. The valley was looted and the winter that followed was remembered for generations.

The nineteenth century brought a different pressure. Schwyz's population grew faster than its thin alpine soil could feed, its cottage textile trade collapsed, and across the nineteenth century the canton had the third-highest emigration rate in Switzerland, behind only Ticino and Obwalden. Josef Anton's move to Thurgau and Karl Anton's crossing to America were not eccentric choices. They were what Schwyz families did.

The crest

It exists. It is in a book in Schwyz.

Switzerland is the most heraldic country in Europe: ordinary farming families, not just nobles, carried arms, and in Schwyz they grew out of the house-marks — simple crosses, hooks and letters — that farmers cut into tools, cheese moulds and boundary stones to say mine. From the fifteenth century, men returning from foreign military service brought back the habit of putting those marks on a shield.

Martin Styger (1856–1935) — lawyer, chancery director, and for three years the canton's own state archivist — spent a lifetime assembling Schwyz heraldry. His Wappenbuch des Kantons Schwyz was published in 1936, the year after he died, edited by his son Paul. It covers every Schwyz Geschlecht — every family holding citizenship in the canton — so Späni is in it. The Canton Schwyz State Archive holds the book and will send a black-and-white copy of a family's entry electronically on request.

What this page will not do is draw you a Späni crest. The blazon is not published online, several heraldry-for-sale sites invent arms for any name typed in, and a family record should carry the real one or none. When the copy from Schwyz arrives, it goes here.

How to get it: email the Staatsarchiv des Kantons Schwyz at afk@sz.ch, ask for the Späni entry from Styger's Wappenbuch des Kantons Schwyz (1936), and mention the family's Bürgerorte of Illgau and Steinen. Written in English is fine; a reply in German is likely.

The village

Illgau is the family's Heimatort — and Späni helped found it

Every Swiss citizen has a Heimatort (also Bürgerort): the commune where the family holds hereditary citizenship, printed on the passport, inherited from father to child regardless of where anyone is actually born or lives. It is a uniquely Swiss idea and it is almost certainly what the family story about a “named village” is remembering. Karl Anton was born in Thurgau, but his Heimatort — and yours, had the line stayed Swiss — was in Schwyz.

The Swiss surname register names the communes where Späni held citizenship: Illgau and Steinen are the two that appear on this family's line — Illgau where five generations were born and married, Steinen where Johann Werner died and where Späni citizenship predates 1800. The full list of Späni communes is in the table below.

And Illgau's own history makes the connection stronger than mere citizenship. The commune's official account of its founding lists eight family names that constituted the entire community — Bürgler, Betschart, Heinzer, Rickenbacher, Horath, Martin, Schuler, and Späni. Your family did not just come from Illgau. It was one of the families Illgau was made of.

Practical consequence: Swiss archives file by Heimatort, not birthplace. The Späni family's civil and citizenship records sit in Schwyz — at the Staatsarchiv and the Illgau and Steinen commune offices — not in Thurgau, where only the 1861 birth entry will be found.

Every later Späni naturalisation — Basel, Lucerne, Zurich, Winterthur, Schaffhausen — traces back to one of these. Not one is in Thurgau. Illgau's Späni citizenship is dated to the 19th century in the register, even though the family is on the parish's founding list; most likely the family's formal citizenship was held at Steinen or another older commune and re-registered at Illgau after it became a political municipality in 1848.

A warning about the FamilySearch surname note. Its dictionary entry for Späni gives a Sardinian Italian origin from spanu, “light grey” or “thinning hair.” That is the etymology of the unrelated Italian surname Spanu/Spano. Swiss Späni is a Schwyz alpine name; no serious source connects the two.

How Späni became Spaeny

One man, five spellings, fifty-seven years

The family story says the name was changed at Ellis Island. The records say something more interesting. Each entry below is a separate document naming Karl Anton or his children; read left to right and you can watch an umlaut dissolve into American paperwork.

Immigration officers never renamed anyone — they copied names off the ship's manifest, which was written in Europe. The change happened afterwards, clerk by clerk, over two generations. The practical lesson: every record search has to cover all five spellings, plus Spani, Spahni and Spöni, the last of which appears on two of Karl Anton's own siblings' infant records back in Switzerland.

The crossing · c. 1878–1882

An undated Atlantic passage, and one record that cannot be right

No arrival record has been identified yet. What bounds it is arithmetic: he was in St. Louis by May 1882, and his mother died in Switzerland in 1880. That puts the crossing in a window of roughly four years — squarely in the Castle Garden era, before Ellis Island's site had even been chosen.

One widely-copied tree records him arriving at Ellis Island in 1909. That cannot be his immigration: by 1909 he had lived in America for twenty-seven years and had seven American-born children. The same 1909 event is attached to Sophia's profile too — the signature of a misfiled record, or, more interestingly, of a couple sailing home to Switzerland and back in middle age. Either way, pulling that manifest is worthwhile: a returning-resident line lists year of first arrival and naturalisation status.

The limit of this record

It carries no unit, no theatre, no decorations

The enlistment file records the day a man entered the Army and nothing after it. “No branch assignment” means precisely that — at induction he had not yet been classified as infantry, artillery, engineers or anything else. So this document cannot say whether he went to Europe, and it lists no medal.

Nothing else in the public record — his obituary, his headstone, his Find a Grave memorial, his Legion and V.F.W. memberships — names a unit, a campaign or a decoration. His marker at Memorial Park reads only Donald K. 1925 – 2011.

Why the file is missing

The 1973 fire at St. Louis

On 12 July 1973 a fire at the National Personnel Records Center destroyed roughly 80 percent of Army personnel files for soldiers discharged between 1 November 1912 and 1 January 1960 — some 16 to 18 million records, with no duplicates and no microfilm. Donald was discharged in 1945 or 1946, squarely inside that window.

This is the single most likely reason no one in the family has his unit or his medals written down. The information existed; the master copy probably burned. What follows is how it gets rebuilt.

How to recover the unit and the medals

Ordered by likelihood of success
  1. Ask the family first. Discharged soldiers were handed a WD AGO Form 53-55, the honourable-discharge certificate. Box 32 lists battles and campaigns; box 33 lists decorations and citations. Families keep these in safe-deposit boxes, Bibles and flag cases far more often than they realise. One photograph of that sheet answers the whole question. Michael in Hutchinson first; then Mark's household in Springfield, which holds nine of Donald's grandchildren and is the likeliest resting place for his papers. Also trace where Alberta's effects went in 2013.
  2. Rice County Register of Deeds, Lyons. Kansas counties routinely recorded veterans' discharge papers, and Registers of Deeds still release them to next of kin. A recorded 53-55 survived the St. Louis fire entirely. Try Barton County (Hoisington) and Reno County (Hutchinson) as well. Rice County Register of Deeds, 101 W Commercial St, Lyons, KS 67554 · 620-257-2931 · rdeeds@ricecounty.us
  3. Kansas National Guard Military Archives, Topeka. They hold World War II Statement of Service documents for 1941–1946 — the pre-DD214 equivalent — and release them to next of kin on an SF-180. Worth a call, with one caveat: their holdings are Kansas National Guard member records, and Donald was inducted as a federal selectee, so he may simply not be in them. Ask before filing. 2722 SW Topeka Blvd, Topeka, KS 66611 · 785-646-0134
  4. File the reconstruction request with NARA anyway. Submit an SF-180 and add NA Form 13055, the questionnaire that lets archivists search auxiliary sources: VA claim files, Multiple Name Pay Vouchers, Selective Service registration, GAO pay records and hospital admissions. Quote the serial number above — 37732174 — which makes the search dramatically more likely to hit. National Personnel Records Center, St. Louis
  5. His VA claim file. Stored separately and untouched by the fire. If he ever claimed a benefit — a home loan, disability, burial allowance — the file summarises his service and usually quotes the discharge document verbatim.
  6. Once a unit is known, the war diary opens up. Regimental and divisional morning reports and after-action reports at NARA College Park record movements day by day, so a unit designation converts directly into the campaigns he was in — and campaign credit is what determines the service stars on his campaign medal.
  7. Legion Post 68 and V.F.W. Post 7428. He was a life member of both and a past Post Commander. Membership applications ask for unit and dates of service, and post historians often keep them. His full obituary in the Hutchinson News, December 2011, may also carry detail the funeral home's online version trimmed.
What this page deliberately does not say. An eighteen-year-old drafted in January 1944 finished basic training around midsummer, precisely when the European theatre was consuming infantry replacements faster than anyone had planned — so European service fits his timeline well, and the family's memory of Europe is entirely credible. But fitting a timeline is not evidence. No document yet places him in Europe, and this page will not name a division, a campaign or a medal for him on inference. Most WWII soldiers received the Victory Medal and a theatre campaign medal more or less automatically, and a confident-sounding list could be produced on that basis — it would be a guess wearing a uniform. Get the 53-55 and the real list will be here, with the service stars that say where he actually was.

A second Spaeny in uniform

The same National Archives file holds one other man of the name: Henry H. Spaeny, Donald's uncle and Charles's youngest son — a Staff Sergeant in the Army Air Corps, born 1898 with nativity recorded as New York, which independently corroborates the family's Brooklyn years. On 1 October 1945, at Smoky Hill Army Air Field in Salina, Kansas, he re-enlisted in the Regular Army under a term recorded as enlistment for the Hawaiian Department. He was forty-seven, married, and signing on for the Pacific six weeks after the war ended. He is buried at Gypsum Hill Cemetery in Salina.

The Swiss graves are gone. Do not expect to find Josef Anton at Zihlschlacht-Sitterdorf or Johann Werner at Steinen. Swiss cemeteries lease plots for a fixed term — typically twenty to twenty-five years — after which the grave is cleared and reused. Nineteenth-century graves survive only as register entries, which is exactly what the research plan goes after. The one exception may be a family memorial plaque inside a church; the Illgau and Steinen parishes are worth asking.

What to do next

Research plan, ordered by what will actually crack

Ranked by the ratio of answer to effort. Track 1 is where a single document would resolve the question the family started with.

1

Date the crossing and find the ship

High odds · days of work
  1. The 1900 federal census, Queens Village. Its columns include year of immigration and naturalisation status. One line of one page dates the crossing to the year. Repeat on 1910, 1920 and 1930 — the answers vary, and the spread itself is informative. Ancestry or FamilySearch, 1900 US Census, Queens County NY · search Spaney / Spane / Spaeni
  2. New York passenger lists, 1877–1882. Search a 16-to-21-year-old Swiss male across all seven spellings, then again with no surname, filtering on age, nationality and year. Castle Garden manifests are thin — often just name, age, occupation, nationality. Free at castlegarden.org; FamilySearch “New York Passenger Lists, 1820–1891”
  3. Naturalisation papers, St. Louis then Rice County. The Declaration of Intention names the ship, the port and the date of arrival in the immigrant's own words. Missouri State Archives; Kansas Historical Society; NARA Kansas City
  4. Pull the 1909 manifest the trees mis-describe. If it lists returning US residents, its columns state the year of first arrival — which answers item 1 outright. Ellis Island records, free at FamilySearch or statueofliberty.org
  5. Ernest's WWI draft card (1917–18) and the indexed 1919 registration, which would settle the 30-versus-31 October birth-date discrepancy.
2

Prove the Swiss generations, get the crest, then push past 1696

Good odds · the registers exist
  1. Email the Staatsarchiv Schwyz for the Späni entry in Styger's Wappenbuch. The single cheapest, highest-satisfaction item on this page. Address and wording are in the crest section above. afk@sz.ch
  2. Work the Schwyz parish registers, not Thurgau. Illgau's own registers run from 1697; anything earlier is in Muotathal's, and Rickenbach falls under Schwyz parish. FamilySearch's “Schweiz, Katholische und Lutherische Kirchenbücher, 1418–1996” is already the source behind generation 5 — the same films should confirm generations 1 through 4. FamilySearch catalogue: Illgau, Muotathal and Schwyz parishes. The Staatsarchiv Schwyz has digitised and microfilmed the baptism, marriage and death books of every parish in the canton — but they are consultable in its reading room only, and staff help is limited, so this is a visit or a hired local researcher. Kollegiumstrasse 30, 6430 Schwyz · staatsarchiv@sz.ch · +41 41 819 20 65
  3. Ask for the Bürgerregister. Swiss communal citizenship registers track families across generations regardless of where members lived — exactly the problem with a family that moved to Thurgau. Request the Späni entries for Illgau and Steinen. Staatsarchiv des Kantons Schwyz; Gemeinde Illgau; Gemeinde Steinen
  4. Find out why Josef Anton left Schwyz. Born Illgau 1813, married Altnau 1860 — a long gap and a long distance. Thurgau's textile and orchard economy drew labour from the inner cantons in exactly that period. Identify Anna Maria Vogelsang's family, who were probably local. Staatsarchiv Thurgau, Frauenfeld
  5. Search Swiss variants too — Spöni and Schöni both appear on Josef Anton's children's records.
  6. Then attack Andreas Späni, d. 1696 — Schwyz parish registers of the 1620s–1690s, in Latin.
3

Fill in the American generations

Certain · the most human material
  1. Sophia Fuhlendorf is a whole second immigrant story. Born 24 November 1857 in Schleswig-Holstein — a duchy Prussia annexed in 1867, when she was nine, which set off heavy emigration. Find her arrival, her parents, and how a Schleswig woman and a Schwyz man met in St. Louis. Start with the parents' names on the 1882 St. Louis marriage record
  2. Explain the Kansas move. Sophia is buried at St. Mary's in Chase, a German-Catholic parish. Chain migration through a church network is the likeliest mechanism — look for other Brooklyn or St. Louis families in the same Rice County townships.
  3. Obituaries, three of them. Charles (April 1939), Sophia (July 1914) and Ernest (May 1964), in the Lyons Daily News and Hutchinson News. Small-town Kansas obituaries of that era routinely name the birthplace village and the year of arrival — the cheapest route to Track 1's answer. Kansas Historical Society newspaper microfilm; Newspapers.com
  4. Find Max's descendants. Max Spaeny of Los Angeles (1867–1949) and his daughter Barbara (1908–1991) are the California branch. The 1910–1940 censuses will show whether Barbara had siblings, and whether any line continues.
  5. Confirm the infant at Alva — “Infant Spaeny,” buried 22 July 1923, plot 06-072-8, three months after Ernest and Ora married there.
  6. Record the living. Michael in Hutchinson, Mark's family in Springfield: Donald's war papers, the Los Angeles years, and whatever Ellis Island story got handed down. Family memory is a source; record it before it thins.
