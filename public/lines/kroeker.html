A new line, and a thin one. This page was started on 7 September 2026 from three facts Shawn supplied — his mother's name, her father's name, and the year his parents married. Everything on it was found the same day. It is four generations deep, not eleven, and it is not dressed up to look otherwise. The research plan says exactly what would deepen it, and most of the answers are currently sitting in the memories of people still living in Hutchinson.

The story in short

What we know, in three paragraphs

The documented line begins with Bueford Lewis Kroeker, born at Hutchinson, Kansas in 1916, and Opal Mae Newburn, born in the same town in 1923 to Leroy and Viola Newburn. They married on 7 June 1942, six months after Pearl Harbor, and they had ten children. Opal was a farmwife at Castleton, a hamlet south-west of Hutchinson, and later a cook at the South Hutchinson sale barn and a cake decorator. They were Baptists, at New Hope. Bueford died in 1993; Opal outlived him by twenty-five years and died in 2018, aged 94, by which time the ten children had become twenty-nine grandchildren, thirty-two great-grandchildren and seven great-great-grandchildren.

Their daughter Twila May Kroeker married Michael Craig Spaeny in 1975. Both families were already in Hutchinson — the Spaenys since 1948, the Newburns and Kroekers for at least a generation before that. Their son is Shawn, who is generation eleven of the Spaeny line and generation four of this one.

What is not yet known is nearly everything above 1916. Bueford's parents are recorded on a submitted family tree as Charles Kroeker and Mary Emma Seaman, with no document behind either name, and the trail stops there. There is also a question hanging over the surname itself: Kroeker is, in origin, a Prussian Mennonite name, and Mennonites settled Reno County in exactly the right period — but this family was Baptist, and its given names look nothing like Mennonite ones. That question is set out below rather than answered.

An open question, stated as one

Where does the name Kroeker come from?

There is a strong, tidy answer available here, and the honest thing to do is lay out both why it is tempting and why it is not yet supportable.

What is documented

Kroeker is a Prussian Mennonite name

The Global Anabaptist Mennonite Encyclopedia records Kroeker first at Tiegenhagen in 1627, in the Mennonite congregations of the Vistula delta — Danzig, Ladekopp, Rosenort, Fürstenwerder, Heubuden, Elbing and Königsberg. From there the name spread with the Mennonite migrations into Poland, into the Russian Empire, and on to North and South America. The etymology is contested: Low German kröke, a bend in the road, or Middle Low German Kröker, a tavern keeper.

And the Kansas geography fits almost too neatly. In 1874 some 1,900 Russian Mennonites took up roughly 60,000 acres across Marion, McPherson, Harvey and Reno counties, bringing the Turkey Red wheat that made the state. The first party found Reno County's crops stripped by grasshoppers and moved east to Harvey County; some came back about ten years later and farmed southern and north-eastern Reno County. Buhler — in Reno County — was founded in the heart of that settlement.

Why this page will not claim it

Three things cut against the tidy answer

The family was Baptist. Opal's obituary puts her at New Hope Baptist Church, not a Mennonite congregation.

The given names are wrong for it. Bueford Lewis and Charles Alford are American names. A Mennonite line of that period runs to Cornelius, Abraham, Peter, Johann, Heinrich.

Seaman is not a Mennonite surname either.

Every one of those is explicable — families left the Mennonite church constantly, and one out-marriage resets a naming pattern completely. But explicable is not evidence. The surname's Mennonite origin is documented; this family's descent from that migration is not. It stays an open question until a record settles it, and the research plan says which record that would be.

How it gets settled: find the parents of Charles Alford Kroeker. If they are Cornelius, Abraham or Johann, the case is close to made; if they are not, the hypothesis is dead. The GRANDMA database (Genealogical Registry and Database of Mennonite Ancestry) and the Mennonite Library and Archives at Bethel College, North Newton — half an hour from Hutchinson — will answer it either way.
Why this matters beyond curiosity. The paternal line spent a century believing an Ellis Island story that turned out to be wrong in its particulars and right in its shape. A Mennonite origin for the Kroekers is exactly the kind of story that could be adopted here — it is plausible, it is flattering, it fits the map — and then be repeated until it hardens into family fact. It gets written down as a question now precisely so that it cannot become an answer by default.

Two lines, one county

Where the lines meet

Hutchinson, Reno County, Kansas · 1975

Not a migration story — a convergence

The Späni–Spaeny line is eleven generations of movement: an alpine parish in Canton Schwyz, then Thurgau, then an Atlantic crossing, St. Louis, Brooklyn, Queens, Kansas, Los Angeles, and Kansas again. It arrives in Hutchinson in 1948, when Donald Spaeny and Alberta Humphrey set up there after marrying at Hoisington.

The Kroekers were already there. Opal Newburn was born in Hutchinson in 1923, twenty-five years before the Spaenys reached it, and her mother had been there long enough to be buried there. So by the time Michael Craig Spaeny married Twila May Kroeker in 1975, both families had been in the same county for a generation. One had travelled six thousand kilometres to get there; the other was already home.

What to do next

Research plan, ordered by what will actually crack

This line is four generations old and stops dead in 1916. Three of these four tracks would each add a generation; the fourth is the one that will not wait.

1

Get Bueford documented

High odds · days of work
  1. Bueford Kroeker's obituary, May 1993, in the Hutchinson News. It should name his parents, his birthplace, his siblings, his church and his burial plot in a single document — converting the whole of generation 1 from a submitted tree into a sourced fact. This is the highest-value document on the line. Reno County Genealogical Society, PO Box 5, Hutchinson KS 67504 · Kansas Historical Society newspaper microfilm · Newspapers.com
  2. His grave at Fairlawn. Not yet located on Find a Grave. The cemetery office can confirm whether he lies with Opal and give the plot for both. Fairlawn Burial Park, 2401 Carey Blvd, Hutchinson KS
  3. The marriage record, 7 June 1942, Reno County. Kansas licences name both sets of parents — an independent check on Charles Kroeker and Mary Emma Seaman. Reno County district court, Hutchinson
  4. Censuses. 1920, 1930 and 1940 for Bueford as a child in Charles's household; the 1950 census, now open and indexed, for Bueford and Opal with their first children.
  5. Bueford's WWII draft registration. Born 1916, he registered. The card gives birth date, birthplace, employer and next of kin — and would settle whether he served.
2

Settle the Kroeker origin question

Decisive either way · do it after track 1
  1. Find the parents of Charles Alford Kroeker (1891–1948). Their given names decide it. Cornelius, Abraham, Johann or Heinrich makes the Mennonite descent probable; anything else kills the hypothesis.
  2. Search GRANDMA — the Genealogical Registry and Database of Mennonite Ancestry, where Kroeker is heavily represented. A hit or a clean miss on a Kansas Charles Kroeker born 1891 answers the question outright.
  3. Mennonite Library and Archives, Bethel College — the repository for the 1874 Kansas migration, thirty miles from Hutchinson. 300 E 27th St, North Newton, KS 67117
  4. If the trail is not Mennonite, treat it as an ordinary German-American line: find the immigrant generation in the 1880–1900 censuses and follow the naturalisation papers.
3

The Newburn and Clune side

Good odds · Clune is rare
  1. Viola Mae Newburn's obituary, March 1980 — it will name Leroy and say whether he was still alive, which is the fastest route to him.
  2. Find Leroy Newburn. No dates, no grave, nothing. Start at Memorial Park, where his wife lies.
  3. Push Viola Mae Clune back. Born 30 May 1903; Clune is an Irish surname and rare in Kansas, which makes her the easiest person on this page to trace to another generation.
  4. Resolve “Ruby Parks.” Opal's obituary names a sister Ruby Parks; Find a Grave has Viola Ruth Newburn Parks (1931–2018), same parents, married name Parks. Probably one woman under a family nickname — but ask, don't assume.
★

Record the living — this one does not wait

Certain · and time-limited
  1. Ask Twila. Her grandparents' names, where the family farmed at Castleton, and — the specific question that matters — whether the word “Mennonite” was ever used in the family. One sentence from her could settle what an archive might take a year to prove.
  2. Ask about Lila and Kenny. Two of Bueford and Opal's ten children, known only as names in a 2022 obituary. Nobody has written down when they were born or when they died.
  3. Ask the surviving siblings. Seven of the ten were living in Hutchinson in 2018. They hold this entire line's memory between them, and there is no fire and no ocean in the way — only time.
  4. Photographs. Bueford died in 1993 and Opal in 2018, so photographs of them certainly exist in the family. That is more than can be said for anyone on the paternal line before 1925, where no image is known to survive of Charles, Sophia, Ernest or Ora. Whatever exists here should be scanned now.
