The Kroeker Line

Five generations of Kroekers in Kansas since 1880, four of them born in Hutchinson — and, through Opal, a Clune line that reaches back to a woman born in New York in 1821. Where the paternal line is a story about leaving, this one is a story about staying put.

A new line, still a thin one. This page was started on 7 September 2026 from three facts Shawn supplied — his mother's name, her father's name, and the year his parents married — and pushed two generations further the same evening. It is five generations deep, not eleven, and above 1916 it rests on other people's family trees rather than on documents. It is not dressed up to look otherwise. The research plan says exactly what would firm it up, and one census page would settle the biggest question on it.

The story in short

What we know, in three paragraphs

The documented line begins with Bueford Lewis Kroeker, born at Hutchinson, Kansas in 1916, and Opal Mae Newburn, born in the same town in 1923 to Leroy and Viola Newburn. They married on 7 June 1942, six months after Pearl Harbor, and they had ten children. Opal was a farmwife at Castleton, a hamlet south-west of Hutchinson, and later a cook at the South Hutchinson sale barn and a cake decorator. They were Baptists, at New Hope. Bueford died in 1993; Opal outlived him by twenty-five years and died in 2018, aged 94, by which time the ten children had become twenty-nine grandchildren, thirty-two great-grandchildren and seven great-great-grandchildren.

Their daughter Twila May Kroeker married Michael Craig Spaeny in 1975. Both families were already in Hutchinson — the Spaenys since 1948, the Newburns and Kroekers for at least a generation before that. Their son is Shawn, who is generation eleven of the Spaeny line and generation five of this one.

Above 1916 the line runs on submitted family trees, not documents. They give Bueford's parents as Charles Alford Kroeker (1891–1948) and Mary Emma Seaman, and Charles's parents as Abraham Kroeker and Johanna Rosina Werrbach, whose eldest son was born in Butler County, Kansas, in January 1880 and who were living in Hutchinson by 1884. There the trail stops. Opal's side goes further: her mother Viola Mae Clune was the daughter of Thomas Charles Clune (1860–1940), and buried near him at Eastside Cemetery is Mary Catherine Wright Clune, born in Rochester, New York in 1821, dead at a hundred and two — probably his mother. And there is still a question hanging over the surname: Kroeker is, in origin, a Prussian Mennonite name, and Abraham is a Mennonite name too — but the family's address in 1880, its children's names, and its church all point the other way. That question is set out below rather than answered.

Gen 1

Abraham Kroeker — and Johanna Rosina Werrbach

dates unknownTree only

The furthest back this line reaches. Submitted trees give Abraham and Johanna six children — Henry (1880–1945), Rosa, Elizabeth, John, Mary (1884–1957) and Charles Alford (1891) — and place the household in Hutchinson city, Ward 5 on the 1900 and 1910 censuses. Henry was born in Butler County, Kansas, on 16 January 1880; Mary in Hutchinson in November 1884. So they were in Kansas by 1880 and in Hutchinson within four years. Where they came from, when they arrived, when they died and where they lie: none of it found.

Abraham is a Mennonite-pattern name, and that matters for the question below. But Johanna Rosina is a south-west German one, and Werbach is a town in Baden-Württemberg. The children's names — Henry, Rosa, Lizzie, John, Mary, Charles — are ordinary German-American. Nothing in this household except the father's first name looks Mennonite.

Gen 2

Charles Alford Kroeker

18 Jul 1891 – 21 Feb 1948Tree only

Born and died at Hutchinson, according to the trees; the youngest of Abraham's six. He married Mary Emma Seaman, about whom nothing at all has been found, and had two children. No document has been seen for his birth, his marriage, his death, or his relationship to Bueford — only user-submitted trees that agree with one another, which is not the same thing. He was born the year before Ellis Island opened and died three years after the Second World War ended.

Converting him from a name into a fact is still the first job on the research plan, and one document — Bueford's 1993 obituary — would probably do it.

Gen 3

Bueford Lewis Kroeker

14 Aug 1916 – 15 May 1993Death documented

Born and died at Hutchinson, Reno County. He married Opal Mae Newburn on 7 June 1942 — a wartime wedding, though whether he served is not yet known — and they farmed at Castleton. He died in 1993 at seventy-six.

The grading is awkward and worth explaining. His death date is documented, because his widow's obituary states it. His birth date and his parents are not — those come from a submitted tree. So the man is real and dated at one end only, and the line above him is guesswork until a record says otherwise.

His name is Bueford, not Buford — the family and every record found so far agree on that, which is worth saying out loud on a website whose other half is largely a story about a name coming apart. Search both anyway; indexes regularise unusual forenames without asking.

Opal Mae Newburn

The best-documented person on this line, and the reason the rest of it exists. Opal's side — the Newburns and the Clunes

Through Opal the line reaches further back, and on firmer ground, than it does through the Kroekers. Her mother's people are on headstones at Eastside Cemetery, and the oldest of them was born in 1821.

Probably Thomas's mother

Mary Catherine Wright Clune

Born 4 March 1821 in Rochester, New York; died in Hutchinson on 14 April 1923, aged 102; buried at Eastside. That she is Thomas Sr.'s mother is inferred — same rare surname, same cemetery, a thirty-nine-year gap — not documented. If it holds, the Clune branch is New York Irish, and this line's earliest date moves back to 1821.

An open question, stated as one

Where does the name Kroeker come from?

There is a strong, tidy answer available here, and the honest thing to do is lay out both why it is tempting and why it is not yet supportable.

What is documented

Kroeker is a Prussian Mennonite name

The Global Anabaptist Mennonite Encyclopedia records Kroeker first at Tiegenhagen in 1627, in the Mennonite congregations of the Vistula delta — Danzig, Ladekopp, Rosenort, Fürstenwerder, Heubuden, Elbing and Königsberg. From there the name spread with the Mennonite migrations into Poland, into the Russian Empire, and on to North and South America. The etymology is contested: Low German kröke, a bend in the road, or Middle Low German Kröker, a tavern keeper.

And the Kansas geography fits almost too neatly. In 1874 some 1,900 Russian Mennonites took up roughly 60,000 acres across Marion, McPherson, Harvey and Reno counties, bringing the Turkey Red wheat that made the state. The first party found Reno County's crops stripped by grasshoppers and moved east to Harvey County; some came back about ten years later and farmed southern and north-eastern Reno County. Buhler — in Reno County — was founded in the heart of that settlement.

Why this page still will not claim it

One thing for, five against

For: Charles's father was named Abraham. That is exactly the test this page set itself in the morning — “if they are Cornelius, Abraham or Johann, the case is close to made.” The evening found an Abraham. Honesty requires saying so.

Against: where he lived. His eldest son was born in Butler County in January 1880. The Russian Mennonites settled Marion, McPherson, Harvey and — from about 1884 — Reno counties. Butler County had German farmers but no Mennonite colony. A Mennonite household that arrived in 1874–78 would not have been there. And in 1900 and 1910 the family was in Hutchinson city, not on the Buhler and Inman farmland where the Mennonites were.

Against: his wife. Johanna Rosina is a Württemberg double name, and Werbach is a town in Baden-Württemberg. If Werrbach is a place-name surname, Johanna's people were south-west German Lutherans or Catholics.

Against: the children. Henry, Rosa, Lizzie, John, Mary, Charles. Not one Low German name among six. Then Charles Alford, Bueford Lewis, and a Baptist church.

So the honest position moved during the day, and it moved away from the tidy answer. Abraham may have been a Mennonite-born man who married out and settled among ordinary German-Americans — that would explain everything — or the name may simply have been in a Prussian Lutheran family too. The surname's Mennonite origin is documented; this family's descent from that migration is not, and it now looks less likely than it did.

How it gets settled: the 1880 federal census for Butler County, Kansas. It records the birthplace of Abraham, of Johanna, and of each of their parents. One page. Free on FamilySearch with a login. If it says Russia or West Prussia, the Mennonite reading revives; if it says Baden, Württemberg, Hesse or Prussia proper, it dies. The GRANDMA database and the Mennonite Library and Archives at Bethel College, North Newton — half an hour from Hutchinson — can then confirm or exclude him by name.
Why this matters beyond curiosity. The paternal line spent a century believing an Ellis Island story that turned out to be wrong in its particulars and right in its shape. A Mennonite origin for the Kroekers is exactly the kind of story that could be adopted here — it is plausible, it is flattering, it fits the map, and now it even has an Abraham in it. It gets written down as a question, with the evidence against it stated as plainly as the evidence for, precisely so that it cannot become an answer by default. There was a Mennonite Kroeker family in Reno County — Peter Kroeker's, from Fuerstenwerder in the Molotschna, who arrived in 1878 and whose son Klaas was elder of Bethel Mennonite Church at Inman for thirty years. Whether they are kin to this Abraham is exactly what nobody yet knows.

Two lines, one county

Where the lines meet

Hutchinson, Reno County, Kansas · 1975

Not a migration story — a convergence

The Späni–Spaeny line is eleven generations of movement: an alpine parish in Canton Schwyz, then Thurgau, then an Atlantic crossing, St. Louis, Brooklyn, Queens, Kansas, Los Angeles, and Kansas again. It arrives in Hutchinson in 1948, when Donald Spaeny and Alberta Humphrey set up there after marrying at Hoisington.

The Kroekers were already there. Opal Newburn was born in Hutchinson in 1923, twenty-five years before the Spaenys reached it, and her mother had been there long enough to be buried there. So by the time Michael Craig Spaeny married Twila May Kroeker in 1975, both families had been in the same county for a generation. One had travelled six thousand kilometres to get there; the other was already home.

What to do next

Research plan, ordered by what will actually crack

This line is five generations deep, and above 1916 it stands on submitted trees rather than documents. Three of these four tracks would each turn a tree into a record; the fourth is the one that will not wait.

1

Get Bueford documented

High odds · days of work
  1. Bueford Kroeker's obituary, May 1993, in the Hutchinson News. It should name his parents, his birthplace, his siblings, his church and his burial plot in a single document — converting the whole of generation 1 from a submitted tree into a sourced fact. This is the highest-value document on the line. Reno County Genealogical Society, PO Box 5, Hutchinson KS 67504 · Kansas Historical Society newspaper microfilm · Newspapers.com
  2. His grave at Fairlawn. Not yet located on Find a Grave. The cemetery office can confirm whether he lies with Opal and give the plot for both. Fairlawn Burial Park, 2401 Carey Blvd, Hutchinson KS
  3. The marriage record, 7 June 1942, Reno County. Kansas licences name both sets of parents — an independent check on Charles Kroeker and Mary Emma Seaman. Reno County district court, Hutchinson
  4. Censuses. 1920, 1930 and 1940 for Bueford as a child in Charles's household; the 1950 census, now open and indexed, for Bueford and Opal with their first children.
  5. Bueford's WWII draft registration. Born 1916, he registered. The card gives birth date, birthplace, employer and next of kin — and would settle whether he served.
2

Settle the Kroeker origin question

One census page · free with a FamilySearch login
  1. The 1880 federal census, Butler County, Kansas. Abraham and Johanna Kroeker with infant Henry. Columns 24–26 give the birthplace of each person and of each person's father and mother. That is the whole question answered on one line. Charles's parents' names have been found — Abraham and Johanna Rosina Werrbach — and the given name Abraham kept the Mennonite reading alive; the Butler County address argues against it. The census breaks the tie. FamilySearch, “United States Census, 1880,” Butler County — free, login required. Also on Ancestry.
  2. Search GRANDMA — the Genealogical Registry and Database of Mennonite Ancestry, where Kroeker is heavily represented. A hit or a clean miss on an Abraham Kroeker with wife Johanna and a son Henry born 1880 answers it outright.
  3. Mennonite Library and Archives, Bethel College — the repository for the 1874 Kansas migration, thirty miles from Hutchinson. 300 E 27th St, North Newton, KS 67117
  4. If the trail is not Mennonite, treat it as an ordinary German-American line: find the immigrant generation in the 1880–1900 censuses and follow the naturalisation papers.
3

The Newburn and Clune side

Good odds · Clune is rare
  1. Viola Mae Newburn's obituary, March 1980 — it will name Leroy and say whether he was still alive, which is the fastest route to him.
  2. Find Leroy Walter Newburn Sr. Full name now known; still no dates, no grave. Start at Memorial Park, where his wife lies. Beware the FamilySearch profile “Leroy Walter Newburn 1925–1991” — that is his son.
  3. Confirm Mary Catherine Wright Clune (1821–1923) as Thomas Sr.'s mother, and find her husband. A Rochester, New York birth in 1821 and a Hutchinson death at 102 make her findable in the 1850–1880 censuses; the 1880 Reno County census should show Thomas, 19, in a Clune household.
  4. Sina M. Clune's maiden name. FamilySearch has a “Bridget Clune (1871–1949)” with identical years — possibly the same woman under her baptismal name. The 1900 census for the Clune household, or Tommy Jr.'s 1895 birth record, would give it.
★

Record the living — this one does not wait

Certain · and time-limited
  1. Ask Twila. Her grandparents' names, where the family farmed at Castleton, and — the specific question that matters — whether the word “Mennonite” was ever used in the family. One sentence from her could settle what an archive might take a year to prove.
  2. Ask about Lila and Kenny. Two of Bueford and Opal's ten children, known only as names in a 2022 obituary. Nobody has written down when they were born or when they died.
  3. Ask the surviving siblings. Seven of the ten were living in Hutchinson in 2018. They hold this entire line's memory between them, and there is no fire and no ocean in the way — only time.
  4. Photographs. Bueford died in 1993 and Opal in 2018, so photographs of them certainly exist in the family. That is more than can be said for anyone on the paternal line before 1925, where no image is known to survive of Charles, Sophia, Ernest or Ora. Whatever exists here should be scanned now.
